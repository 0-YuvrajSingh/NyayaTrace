Executive Summary This project studies how Artificial Intelligence can assist legal   research in the Indian legal system while remaining explainable,   evidence-grounded, verifiable, and subject to human control. The   proposed system is an Explainable Legal Research and Decision-Support   Framework rather than an autonomous judge or lawyer. The primary task is legal research and evidence-backed case analysis:   given a legal question or case facts, the system retrieves relevant   statutes and precedents, selects supporting passages, verifies citation   identity and provenance, and produces a structured explanation that a   legal professional can inspect. Court-outcome prediction is retained as   a secondary, measurable capability rather than the identity of the   project. Core research question: Can an evidence-grounded and   provenance-constrained AI framework improve the reliability and   explainability of Indian legal research compared with an AI workflow   that is not explicitly grounded in verified legal evidence? The project will use Indian legal cases and associated authority   information, with ILDC as the principal academic benchmark where   applicable. The system will combine a traditional benchmark, a   legal-language baseline, BM25 retrieval, evidence selection,   citation/provenance tracking, a controlled generation layer where   feasible, and a structured explanation surface. Temporal eligibility of   legal evidence will be implemented as a reliability constraint for   historical evaluation, but it is not the primary novelty claim.   Multilingual support is explicitly deferred to future work. This document is the frozen semester scope. It prioritizes a defensible   research contribution, measurable reliability constraints, and a   reproducible implementation over feature breadth. Problem Legal AI introduces a reliability problem that differs from ordinary   text-generation systems. A fluent model can produce a convincing legal   explanation while citing an incorrect case, inventing a paragraph,   relying on evidence that was not retrieved, or presenting a weak   authority as if it were decisive. These failures are particularly   serious in law because a material claim must be inspectable against a   legal source. The Indian context makes this concrete. In Pooja Ramesh Singh v. Jammu   and Kashmir Bank Ltd. (2026 INSC 668), the Supreme Court addressed   reliance on AI-generated fake and hallucinated case law and the   consequences for judicial decision-making. The judgment provides a   current motivation for verification, provenance, and human oversight.   [1] The project therefore treats explainability as a system property, not as   a cosmetic paragraph added after generation. A useful output must   connect material claims to retrieved evidence and preserve enough   provenance for a reviewer to reproduce the source path. Research Gap Prior Indian legal-NLP work has already demonstrated court judgment   prediction and explanation. ILDC introduced a large Indian Supreme Court   corpus and a Court Judgment Prediction and Explanation task,   establishing that legal prediction and explanation are feasible but not   equivalent to expert legal reasoning. [2] The proposed project does not claim novelty for Legal-BERT, BM25, RAG,   court judgment prediction, or explainability individually. The research   gap targeted here is the integration and controlled evaluation of   multiple reliability constraints in an Indian legal research workflow:   evidence grounding, citation/provenance restriction, temporal   eligibility for historical evaluation, and a legally meaningful   explanation surface. The contribution is therefore a measurable framework and experimental   study rather than a claim that the underlying AI techniques are new. Final Contribution The project will contribute a reproducible Indian legal XAI framework   that produces a research output only after retrieving relevant   authorities, selecting evidence from that retrieval set, attaching   provenance metadata, validating citation identity/source path, and   generating a structured explanation of the evidence used. Outcome   prediction is evaluated as one capability of the framework, not as the   sole system objective. Research Questions Only the following three research questions are in scope. RQ1 — Does grounding an Indian legal AI workflow in retrieved legal   evidence improve legal-research reliability and relevant-evidence   retrieval compared with a facts-only legal-language baseline? RQ2 — Does provenance-constrained evidence selection and citation   verification reduce unsupported or unverifiable legal claims in the   final output? RQ3 — Can the proposed structured explanation format improve   human-verifiable transparency without materially degrading prediction or   retrieval performance? Hypotheses H1 — Evidence-grounded retrieval will improve relevant-evidence   retrieval and will provide a stronger basis for legal-research outputs   than a facts-only legal-language baseline. H2 — Provenance-constrained citation and verification will reduce   unsupported or unverifiable citations compared with an unconstrained   generation/evidence-selection condition. H3 — Structured, evidence-linked explanations will receive higher   human-rated transparency and verifiability than outputs that provide a   conclusion without an explicit evidence path, subject to reviewer   availability. Research Objectives Develop a reproducible AI pipeline for Indian legal case analysis   using a legal-language model and an explicit legal retrieval layer. Ground material legal claims in retrieved legal passages rather than   relying only on model-internal knowledge. Implement citation provenance so every final cited authority can be   traced to a concrete source document and passage. Implement temporal eligibility as a historical-evaluation integrity   constraint so later legal information does not enter historical test   cases. Generate structured explanations showing the issue, authority,   evidence, and conclusion path without exposing private model   chain-of-thought. Evaluate retrieval quality, citation correctness, provenance,   temporal integrity, explanation quality, and secondary   outcome-prediction performance. Define governance controls for human oversight, privacy,   auditability, contestability, and non-autonomous use. Temporal effectiveness A statute/provision is treated as effective   only during its defined validity interval when   reliable metadata is available. Temporal applicability Whether the frozen historical-eligibility rule   ( Eligible(e,t) ) permits the item for the   evaluation case. Provenance validity The final citation maps to an identified source   document and exact passage that was retrieved   for the case/query. These definitions must be frozen before final test evaluation and may   not be changed after inspecting results. Temporal Integrity Protocol Temporal eligibility is included as an integrity control for historical   evaluation, not as the primary novelty claim. A historical case should   not be evaluated using later legal information when the experimental   question is what the system could have used at the case decision time. Statute passages should carry effective and end-validity metadata   where available. Precedent eligibility should require decision_date <= case_date,   with the target case and duplicates/near-duplicates excluded. Unresolved temporal metadata must follow a predefined rule and must   not be manually corrected after observing test results. Current-law deployment is conceptually different from historical   prediction and is not a separate semester benchmark. Explainability Framework Explainability is defined in legal terms rather than only as   model-internal feature attribution. The system should answer four   questions for a reviewer: what did the system output, which evidence   supports it, why was that evidence selected, and where exactly can the   reviewer verify it. Evaluation Reference Evidence Retrieval and citation evaluation must use a predefined reference   evidence set or defensible authority mapping available before final test   evaluation. The reference must not be constructed retrospectively from   the system’s own retrieved output. If existing ILDC   authority/explanation annotations are used, the mapping procedure must   be documented and frozen before final scoring. [2] Mandatory Error Analysis E2 wrong, E3/E4 correct: evidence retrieval contributed useful   information. E3 correct, E4 wrong: evidence constraints or selection removed   useful support. E3/E4 prediction correct but citation unsupported: prediction and   evidence quality diverge. Correct authority retrieved but final answer wrong: retrieval was   sufficient but reasoning/prediction failed. Citation is traceable but authority-consistency fails: provenance   succeeded without proving legal relevance. Citation is later than the historical case date: temporal integrity   failure. Explanation highlights text that does not correspond to the   retrieved supporting evidence: explanation faithfulness failure. Responsible AI and Governance Requirements The system is a decision-support research prototype. It must be designed   around human oversight, transparency, accountability, privacy,   contestability, and non-autonomous use. NITI Aayog’s Responsible AI work   identifies safety and reliability, equality,   inclusivity/non-discrimination, privacy/security, transparency,   accountability, and protection of positive human values as guiding   principles. [3] The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 is a key privacy-law   source for analysing personal-data handling in any deployment context,   while its application and exemptions must be assessed against the   specific use case rather than assumed. [4] Control Required treatment Human oversight Every consequential legal output is   labelled as AI assistance and   requires human verification. No autonomous decision No autonomous judicial, sentencing,   bail, guilt, or final legal   decision function. Citation verification A final output cannot introduce a   legal authority outside the   verified retrieval/provenance set. Auditability Log model version, retrieval   configuration, evidence IDs,   relevant input/output metadata, and   experiment IDs. Privacy Minimize stored personal data;   apply access control and   redaction/pseudonymisation where   appropriate. Bias/fairness Measure error patterns and subgroup   differences when ethically and   legally appropriate and when the   dataset supports such evaluation. Uncertainty Do not equate model confidence with   legal certainty; disclose   limitations. Contestability Reviewer must be able to reject an   evidence item, challenge an output,   and inspect the source passage. Dataset and Corpus Plan ILDC is the principal academic benchmark because it was designed for   Indian Supreme Court judgment prediction and explanation and provides   outcome and authority/explanation information useful for controlled   experiments. [2] The exact usable subset, split, labels, and   licensing/access constraints must be verified during Weeks 1–2 before   final preprocessing is frozen. The retrieval corpus should be built from legally attributable sources   and should preserve document identity, authority metadata, dates, and   passage boundaries. No corpus expansion should be added merely to   increase feature count; each added source type must have a clear   research role. Scope Freeze and Change Control The research scope is frozen around the evidence-grounded, explainable   legal AI framework for legal research and decision support. The   following changes are not permitted during the core semester execution   merely because they appear attractive: new model families, multilingual   support, large application features, graph architectures, additional   research questions, or unrelated benchmark tasks. Changes are permitted only when technically necessary to repair a core   component while preserving the research question and evaluation design.   Any such change must be documented in the reproducibility record. Final One-Paragraph Summary This project proposes an Explainable AI Framework for Indian legal   research in which a legal question or historical case is processed   through a legal-language model and an explicit legal retrieval pipeline.   BM25 retrieves relevant statutes and precedent passages; an   evidence-selection layer chooses support; provenance metadata records   the exact source and passage; citation validation rejects unsupported   authorities; temporal eligibility prevents later information from   entering historical evaluation; and an explanation layer exposes the   evidence basis of the final output. The core evaluation compares a   traditional predictive baseline, a facts-only legal-language baseline, a   retrieval-grounded legal research system, and the full   provenance-constrained explainable framework using retrieval, citation,   grounding, temporal-integrity, provenance, explanation, and secondary   outcome-prediction metrics. The project does not attempt to replace   judges or lawyers, and multilingual support is intentionally deferred to   future work. Research Basis / References [1] Supreme Court of India.
